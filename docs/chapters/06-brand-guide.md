# A brand someone else can build

You have done the work. The archetype is chosen, the persuasion is honest, the visual direction sings — in your head. Then you hand the project to a partner, and they ask the questions your head never had to answer out loud: *Which blue? How much space around the logo? What does "confident" sound like in a size-chart caption?*

A brand guide is the answer written down. It turns a private picture into decisions another person — a partner, a developer, an AI tool — can execute without you standing beside them.

## Strategy first, style second

A guide that starts with colors has skipped the foundation. Begin with four short statements:

- **Audience** — who this is for, described by what they want and believe, not just demographics.
- **Promise** — the one thing this brand commits to deliver, every time.
- **Positioning** — the place you want in the audience's understanding, relative to the alternatives: the simplest choice, the most considered choice, the loudest refusal.
- **Voice** — how the brand sounds, stated as rules a stranger could follow: short sentences, concrete nouns, no superlatives, humor allowed in captions but never in checkout.

Every visual decision downstream becomes checkable against these four. "Does this photography fit?" stops being a matter of taste and becomes a matter of record.

## Style, as named decisions

Then the visible layer — each choice named so it can be reused and discussed:

- **Typography** — the typefaces, the scale, the line lengths. Not "clean fonts" but: *this face, these sizes, these weights, headings never in all caps.*
- **Color** — a small palette with roles: this color acts, this color informs, this color decorates. The [U.S. Web Design System's design tokens](https://designsystem.digital.gov/design-tokens/) demonstrate the principle — repeated style decisions become named values like "primary" or "accent" — without dictating your identity or stack.
- **Spacing and layout** — the grid, the margins, the rhythm. White space is a decision, not an accident.
- **Imagery** — what photography and illustration look like here: lighting, cropping, who appears, what never appears.

A **design token** — a named, reusable style value — is the smallest unit of this chapter. `--accent` is a brand decision someone else can build with.

## Show the rule, and where it stops

Rules without examples invite interpretation; examples without rules invite imitation. A good guide pairs them: *the voice is plain — like this product caption, not like this one.* And it marks the boundaries: which decisions are fixed (the palette), which are flexible (layout within the grid), and which are open (the photographer's eye). A guide that fixes everything leaves no room for the collaborator's judgment; a guide that fixes nothing was never written.

## A mini guide for the shirt

Here is the invented **Standard Issue** brand, compressed to five lines — proof of how small a useful guide can be:

> **Promise:** one good basic, no decisions. **Voice:** declarative, concrete, dry humor allowed, never urgency. **Type:** one grotesque sans, two weights. **Color:** white, ink, one red accent reserved for actions only. **Imagery:** single garment, flat daylight, no props, no models' faces.

Hand those five lines to ten different people — or one AI tool — and you will get ten recognizably related results. That is what a guide is for.

## The handoff

The final test of a guide is a handoff you do not attend. Your partner builds a page while you are away. Your AI assistant drafts an email from a prompt quoting the voice rules. The work comes back, and it sounds like the brand. When that happens, the idea has stopped living in your head — and started living in the system.

**A question to carry into the last chapter:** you can now define a brand on paper. What changes when the paper becomes a screen — when the page can respond, scroll, be searched, and be read aloud by a machine?
