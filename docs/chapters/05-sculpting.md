# Sculpting with AI

"Make it better" is the most common prompt in the world, and the least useful. It hands every decision back to the machine — what *better* means, what to keep, what to cut. The output will be plausible, smooth, and nobody's.

Now compare: *"Keep the Explorer story. Remove the luxury cues. Make the headline sound curious rather than boastful. Three alternatives. Do not add product claims."* Same machine, same request for change — but this time a person is sculpting. The model supplies the material; you decide what the work is becoming.

## The block and the cuts

Think of a first AI draft the way a sculptor thinks of a block: full of potential shapes, most of them wrong. Your vocabulary from the last three chapters is the toolset:

- **Persuasion** lets you say *which* response to sharpen: "this answers the fit question but never builds trust — strengthen the maker's voice."
- **Archetype** lets you hold the story constant while everything else moves: "keep the Sage, drop the swagger."
- **Visual language** lets you direct images and layouts: "aligned, generous white space, one accent color" versus "layered, cropped mid-gesture, clashing scales."

A prompt made of these words is not a wish. It is an instruction.

## A working session

An invented session with our invented shirt, four cuts deep:

**Cut 1 — generate contrast.** *"Write three 50-word descriptions of a plain white cotton T-shirt: one Explorer, one Sage, one Rebel. State product facts only: 100% cotton, machine washable, made in Portugal."* Three directions, cheap to compare. You are not choosing copy yet — you are choosing a story.

**Cut 2 — name what works.** You read the three and say which and why: "The Sage version respects the reader, but it lectures. The Explorer opening line is right." Naming what works before asking for changes is the single highest-leverage habit. If you cannot say what is good, you cannot ask for more of it.

**Cut 3 — change one thing.** *"Keep the Explorer opening and the product facts. Rewrite the rest: shorter sentences, remove 'journey' and 'adventure,' end with a practical detail instead of a promise."* One cut, then inspect. Changing five things at once leaves you guessing which change did the work.

**Cut 4 — protect the facts.** *"List every factual claim in the current version. Mark any you invented."* The machine will happily embroider a fiber blend that does not exist. The human verifies. That is the job.

## Keep the good versions

Exploration destroys value if it erases the best draft along the way. Keep versions — in files, in commits, anywhere durable — so a wrong turn is a branch, not a loss. (This book itself works that way: every chapter travels through version control, and any earlier state can be recovered.)

## Where judgment stays

The machine can generate twenty headlines. It cannot tell you whether any of them should exist — whether the urgency is honest, whether the archetype fits the product you actually sell, whether the joke lands in a culture you do not know. Taste, ethics, and truthfulness are not features of the model. They are the contribution of the person holding the tools.

**A question to carry into the next chapter:** one person sculpting a draft is a skill. Two people building the same brand — with the same machine between them — is a coordination problem. How do you write your decisions down so someone else can make them?
