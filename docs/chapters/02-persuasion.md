# Why we say yes

Two signs hang beside two identical shirts — invented, both of them.

The first reads: *"Not sure about fit? Measurements for every size, model measurements included."* The second reads: *"Only 3 left — offer ends at midnight."* Both are trying to move you toward the same button. But notice how differently they treat you. The first assumes you have a real question and answers it. The second assumes you can be hurried.

Persuasion starts with that difference. Before choosing any technique, ask what kind of decision you are helping someone make — and whether you are helping at all.

## Seven levers, one source

Psychologist Robert Cialdini spent years studying the people who persuade for a living — salespeople, fundraisers, advertisers — and distilled what he saw into a small set of principles. His [own overview](https://www.influenceatwork.com/7-principles-of-persuasion/) names seven:

- **Reciprocity** — we tend to return what we receive. A genuinely useful size guide can create goodwill before any purchase.
- **Commitment and consistency** — we like our actions to match our stated selves. "Build your weekly uniform" invites a small yes that grows.
- **Social proof** — when uncertain, we look around. Reviews and real photos borrow confidence from the crowd.
- **Authority** — we defer to credible expertise. A maker explaining fabric weight carries weight of their own.
- **Liking** — we say yes to people and brands we like. Warmth, similarity, and honest charm are not decoration; they are persuasion.
- **Scarcity** — rare things feel more valuable. Sometimes true (a limited run); sometimes manufactured (a timer that resets at midnight).
- **Unity** — shared identity: "people like us wear things like this." The strongest lever, and the easiest to fake.

Treat this list as the theorist's account of his own framework. Particular findings — how strong an effect is, when it fails — belong to the original studies, and honest use of the model means following the trail back to them rather than quoting the overview as proof.

## Framing: the same fact, made relevant

In a classic research program, Tversky and Kahneman showed that people respond differently to the *same* information depending on how it is framed — a disease program described in lives saved versus lives lost produces different choices ([the original article](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.7455683)).

The lesson for our shirt is not "frames trick people." It is that **no presentation is neutral**. "100% cotton" and "no synthetics" state the same fact and invite different thoughts. The persuader's first ethical act is noticing that a frame was chosen — by you.

## The shirt, four ways

One invented shirt, four invented invitations. Watch which lever each pulls:

| Presentation | Lever | The invitation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| "The last white tee you'll need to think about" | Relief, consistency | Decide once, be done |
| "Worn by 40,000 people who hate shopping" | Social proof | The crowd already checked |
| "Cut and sewn by people we name on this page" | Authority, liking | Trust the makers |
| "A run of 200. When they're gone, they're gone." | Scarcity | Act while it exists |

None of these change the fiber. All of them change what the fiber *means* — and how it feels to say yes.

## Where persuasion ends

Persuasion respects the freedom to say no. Manipulation removes it: hidden costs, fake timers, invented reviews, pressure disguised as information. The practical test is simple. If your reader knew exactly what you were doing and why, would they still thank you for it?

That test travels into every prompt. "Write an ad that creates urgency" is a different ethical object than "write an ad that answers the fit question clearly." Both are persuasion. Only one deserves the name.

**A question to carry into the next chapter:** persuasion explains why someone might say yes. It does not explain who they get to *be* when they do. That is where archetypes come in.
