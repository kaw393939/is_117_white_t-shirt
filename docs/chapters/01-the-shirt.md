# The shirt stays. The story changes.

Imagine three shop windows on the same street.

In the first window, a white T-shirt hangs alone on a wooden hanger, evenly lit, the price printed in small type. Nothing competes with it. In the second, the same style of shirt lies half-folded on a paper map, next to a compass and a train ticket. In the third, the shirt is pinned to a corkboard, flecked with paint, surrounded by zines and a hand-lettered sign that seems to argue with the idea of shopping at all.

These windows are invented — you will not find them on any street. But the question they raise is real: the shirt never changed, so what did?

## A fact and a story

Hold a plain white T-shirt and you hold two kinds of information at once.

Some of it is **product fact**: the fiber, the stitching, the cut, the weight of the cloth. Facts can be checked. They stay true no matter who is looking.

The rest is **story**: what the shirt means, who it is for, what it says about the person wearing it. A story cannot be checked the way a fiber-content label can. A story is built — with words, images, prices, shop windows, and now with prompts.

This book is about learning to build the story on purpose, while keeping the facts straight.

## Three lenses

Three vocabularies do most of the work, and each gets a chapter:

- **Persuasion** asks what response we are inviting. Attention? Trust? A click? [Chapter 2](02-persuasion.md) gives this a language.
- **Archetype** asks what identity the shirt is expressing. The reliable essential? The explorer's companion? The rebel's uniform? [Chapter 3](03-archetypes.md) shows how flexible — and how slippery — these patterns can be.
- **Visual language** asks how that identity should look and feel. Orderly grid or noisy collage? [Chapter 4](04-visual-language.md) traces two design traditions and the wide space between them.

## One shirt, three tellings

Consider three invented presentations of the same shirt. The brands are made up; the mechanics are not.

**Standard Issue** presents the shirt as the end of decision-making: one good basic, easily replaced, never out of place. The persuasion leans on relief. The archetype leans on the dependable everyperson. The visuals stay quiet — one typeface, generous white space, no exclamation points.

**Drift & Thread** presents the same shirt as a travel companion: pack light, wash it in a sink, wear it everywhere. The persuasion leans on possibility. The archetype is the explorer. The photography is warm, creased, and halfway out the door.

**Blank Canvas Co.** presents the shirt as a joke about ordinary products: the garment is serious, while the page refuses to take the transaction too seriously. The persuasion leans on recognition and amusement. The archetype is the Jester. The visuals argue — clashing type, rough crops, deliberate noise.

Same fiber. Same cut. Three different invitations to say yes.

## The prompt is a cut

Now watch what happens when this thinking meets an AI tool.

A vague prompt — *"write an ad for a white T-shirt"* — hands every decision to the model. You will get something plausible and generic, because you supplied no intent.

A precise prompt makes deliberate cuts:

> Write a 60-word product description for a plain white cotton T-shirt. Invite a feeling of relief from too many choices. Speak as a dependable, unfussy companion. Use short sentences and concrete nouns — no exclamation points, no superlatives. Do not invent product facts.

The output changed because the intent changed. [Chapter 5](05-sculpting.md) treats this as sculpting: the AI's first draft is a block of material, and each instruction removes a little more of what the work is not.

## What stayed true

Through every window and every prompt, the shirt stayed the shirt. That is the discipline this book practices: change the presentation freely, label what is invented, and never let a good story falsify a plain fact.

**A question to carry into the next chapter:** the last time you bought something ordinary, which parts were the thing — and which parts were the telling?
