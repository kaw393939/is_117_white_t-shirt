## Typography: a voice is also a reading condition

Butterick foregrounds font, size, line spacing and line length together [VF-01]. That gives the chapter a better opening than a shopping list of typefaces: keep the words and change the conditions under which someone meets them.

**Original fictional comparison for #110:** set “A white shirt. Nothing accidental.” above the same product facts in three treatments. First vary only the headline's typeface; next retain the face and vary scale; finally revise the body text's line length and spacing. Label each changed variable. Do not call the treatments objectively trustworthy, rebellious or premium: those are proposed readings to explain and investigate.

Teach vocabulary at the moment it becomes useful: *hierarchy* names what receives emphasis; *line length* names the width of the reading run; *leading* names line spacing; *tracking* changes spacing across a run, while *kerning* concerns particular character pairs. Have the eventual figure expose those changes rather than decorate a glossary with unconnected labels. Technical definitions and font licensing need checking against the chosen font's documentation during #110.

*Helvetica* belongs beside this comparison as a conversation about the meanings people attach to type, not as a verdict that one face is correct. Hustwit's page verifies the film's identity and participants [VF-02]. For #67/#125, inspect an authorized copy before adding speaker positions, quotations or timestamps. Link to the filmmaker; do not imply that access includes rights to reproduce stills or screen publicly.

## Color: resemblance, meaning and measurement

The Albers Foundation describes a teaching method built around observing color in context [VF-03]. Our original comparison can put identical color chips against two different surrounds, then reveal the unchanged values. It is a demonstration to inspect, not a promise that every viewer will report an identical effect. Do not reproduce an Albers plate or label an invented diagram as his work.

Keep a separate question beside it: can someone read the product information? WCAG 2.2 SC 1.4.3 sets minimum contrast of 4.5:1 for ordinary text and 3:1 for qualifying large text, with specified exceptions. The large-text definition includes 18-point text or 14-point bold text. Logotypes have an exception, but ordinary brand-colored copy does not inherit it. SC 1.4.1 prohibits color as the sole visual means of communicating information [VF-04]. Meeting these two criteria alone is not full WCAG conformance.

**Original fictional comparison for #111:** keep the garment white and change the surrounding storefront palette. Separate decorative accents from body text, links and errors. Record exact foreground/background values and computed contrast for each text pair. Give an error a visible written message, not just a red border. Include a textual explanation of the color experiment so the lesson does not depend entirely on perceiving its effect.

“This red recalls a poster I grew up with” is a personal explanation. “Red makes everyone buy” is an empirical claim requiring evidence we do not have. The former can support an intentional choice without pretending to prove the latter.

## Grids and proportion: a constraint, not an alibi

For this book, introduce a grid operationally: shared alignment lines, columns and gaps that help organize repeated information. The original storefront can expose those guides, then hide them. CSS Grid provides a technical route to rows and columns; its specification also distinguishes visual rearrangement from logical order [VF-05]. A pleasing composition does not excuse an incoherent reading or keyboard sequence.

Euclid's definition relates a whole to its larger segment as that segment relates to the smaller [VF-06]. Let the larger-to-smaller ratio be r. Then r = 1 + 1/r, so r² − r − 1 = 0 and the positive solution is (1 + √5)/2, approximately 1.618. This derivation is ours, not a quotation from Euclid.

**Original fictional comparison for #112:** divide a 1,000-unit usable content width, excluding the gutter, three ways: 500/500, 600/400 and approximately 618/382. Use the same shirt image, product facts and action. Label the last split an intentionally constructed golden-ratio approximation. Compare available text space, image emphasis and narrow-screen behavior. No variant wins because its numbers have an ancient pedigree.

The definition supplies no evidence that a particular building, painting or logo was designed using that ratio. Do not overlay a spiral on an artifact and present the fit as historical discovery. Claims of intention require documentary evidence; claims of universal preference require appropriate empirical evidence. Neither is established here. The chapter can teach the proportion without either claim.

Greiman's museum-hosted testimony offers the stronger human story. Our editorial connection is to the author's argument about tools extending imagination. It is an analogy, not evidence that early digital composition and generative AI operate alike or raise identical questions of authorship. Verify any quotation attributed within her recollection separately before treating it as the original speaker's words.

## The mark can move

**Original fictional comparison for #113:** keep the white garment and the [canonical product facts](../product-record.md) fixed. Move one invented identifier from chest to sleeve to hem; then compare small and large versions in the same location. Show the whole garment as well as detail crops so a close-up cannot disguise scale. Annotate intended viewing distance, relationship to seams, brand rationale and what remains unknown.

A hem mark might serve the wearer discovering it; a large front mark might address someone across a room. Those are proposed intentions, not measured customer responses. Neither is automatically subtler, more ethical or more on-brand. Printed and embroidered trials require actual production evidence before we claim minimum usable sizes, wash durability, cost or manufacturing feasibility. Screen mockups alone cannot settle those questions.
