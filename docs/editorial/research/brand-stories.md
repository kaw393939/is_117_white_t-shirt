## Editorial selection

Use Starbucks to connect literature, naming, symbol, scale, color and consistency. Use March of Dimes to ask what a name means when an organization’s purpose changes. These cases offer different questions; neither needs a fixed archetype label before it can teach something useful. The relationship to Mark and Pearson’s analysis must be checked against the actual passages before attributing their interpretation.

## Case A: a literary name on an everyday purchase

**Documented starting point:** Starbucks says its name draws on Moby-Dick. Its founders page also describes writing and teaching among the founders’ backgrounds. [BS-03](https://about.starbucks.com/about-us/), [BS-04](https://about.starbucks.com/history/our-founders/).

**Our teaching interpretation:** a name can import associations from another cultural setting without depicting the product literally. That is an opportunity to discuss how literature becomes design material. The association still has to work for people who have never read the book.

**Possible story opening, written for this book:** “A cup of coffee sends us to a novel. Before the drink has a taste, its name has already brought another world into the transaction.” This is editorial narration, not a quotation or a report of a customer’s thoughts.

**Return to the shirt (invented exercise):** propose three names drawn from different personal or cultural references. Explain each association, check unintended meanings, and compare the names without explaining the references first. Record what actual readers say if a session occurs; until then, responses remain unknown.

**Counterquestion:** does the name communicate something useful on its own, or does the explanation do all the work? A reference that matters to its maker may be opaque to its audience.

## Case B: the mark changes when its surface changes

Starbucks’ logo history links a change in the Siren’s depiction to enlarging the mark for delivery trucks. The same company account describes successive revisions and the removal of the name in the 2011 version. Attribute the account to Starbucks rather than describing its anecdote as independently verified. [BS-05](https://about.starbucks.com/history/the-evolution-of-our-logo/).

**Our teaching interpretation:** scale and surface are part of the message. Enlarging a mark can change what people notice. The company’s public guide also provides an example of a symbol and wordmark having different roles, although current guide details need a direct-page recheck. [BS-06](https://creative.starbucks.com/logos/).

**Return to the shirt (invented exercise):** create an original identifier and compare it at the hem, on the sleeve and across the chest. Keep the design itself fixed for the first comparison. Then simplify it for the smallest placement and explicitly identify what changed. Explain which version can be recognized, read or appreciated at its intended distance. Do not copy the Siren onto the fictional shirt.

**Counterquestion:** an established brand’s ability to omit its name does not demonstrate that an unfamiliar shirt brand can do the same successfully. Recognition is a question to investigate.

## Case C: a stable identity with room to vary

The official color guide describes both a recurring green presence and expressive seasonal colors. [BS-07](https://creative.starbucks.com/color/).

**Our teaching interpretation:** a brand guide can define what remains recognizable while permitting change. The reader can inspect an actual design system instead of treating “consistency” as a demand for identical compositions.

**Return to the shirt (invented exercise):** preserve one identifier and one color role while varying photography, headline size and accent colors across three campaign pages. Explain which elements carry recognition and which carry the occasion. Do not claim this experiment proves increased recognition without observation.

## Case D: what remains when a mission changes?

March of Dimes provides a different question about continuity. The organization’s history dates a new mission announcement to 1958; its naming history recounts the fundraising phrase associated with Eddie Cantor. Mark and Pearson’s publisher lists a dedicated March of Dimes chapter, but that listing is not enough to summarize their analysis. [BS-09](https://www.marchofdimes.org/about-us/mission/history/history-march-dimes), [BS-10](https://www.marchofdimes.org/about-us/mission/history/origin-our-name), [BS-01](https://www.mheducation.com/highered/mhp/product/hero-outlaw-building-extraordinary-brands-through-power-archetypes.html).

**Our teaching question:** how can a name preserve recognition while the organization changes what it asks people to support? The museum envelope offers a possible artifact to inspect after its details and reproduction conditions are checked. [BS-11](https://americanhistory.si.edu/collections/object/nmah_1216347).

**Return to the shirt (invented exercise):** the fictional store begins offering repairs alongside shirts. Identify what the name, navigation and brand promise should retain and what must change to make the new service understandable. No historical claim is made that this retail scenario is equivalent to the organization’s mission transition.
